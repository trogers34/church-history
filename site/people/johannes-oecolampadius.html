Johannes Oecolampadius

1482–1531 · Reformation

Also known as Johannes Hussgen (or Heussgen); Johann Oekolampad

Humanist scholar and Reformer of Basel; Zwingli's closest ally

A gifted scholar of Greek and Hebrew who helped Erasmus with his Greek New Testament and translated the Church Fathers, Oecolampadius became the leading Reformer of Basel. He argued that Christ's words 'This is my body' should be read figuratively, defended that view with Zwingli against Luther at Marburg in 1529, and led Basel to abolish the Mass the same year. He pressed for church discipline run by the church rather than the city council, an idea that influenced Calvin. He supported the council's measures against Catholic worship and against the Anabaptists.

What they did

  • Led the Reformation in Basel, which formally abolished the Mass and adopted a Reformed church order in 1529.[1][2]
  • Argued from the Church Fathers that Christ's presence in the Lord's Supper is spiritual and the words 'This is my body' figurative, becoming with Zwingli a chief opponent of Luther in the Supper controversy.[3][5]
  • Defended the Reformed view with Zwingli against Luther and Melanchthon at the Marburg Colloquy (1529), where the sides agreed on fourteen of fifteen articles but not on the Supper.[2][4]
  • Argued that church discipline and excommunication should be in the hands of church elders rather than the city council, an idea later developed by Calvin in Geneva.[2][3]
  • Assisted Erasmus with the Hebrew in his Greek New Testament (1516) and translated many works of the Greek Fathers, especially Chrysostom, into Latin.[2][5]
  • Wrote biblical commentaries on Isaiah, Romans, and other books, and was an influential Hebrew scholar.[2][5]
  • Married the widow Wibrandis Rosenblatt, who after his death married Wolfgang Capito and then Martin Bucer.[2]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Oecolampadius debated the Anabaptists rather than urging their execution, but he supported the Basel council's laws suppressing Catholic worship and punishing Anabaptists with imprisonment and banishment, and argued that the government should enforce true religion.

1529–1531

Abolition of the Mass in Basel

Approved or defended it

Against: Catholic clergy and worshippers in Basel

After iconoclastic riots in February 1529, the Basel council, with Oecolampadius as chief preacher, abolished the Mass and removed images, and its Reformation Ordinance required attendance at Reformed services. Catholic canons and scholars, including Erasmus, left the city.[1][2]

Outcome: Catholic worship was banned in Basel.

1525–1531

Measures against the Anabaptists

Approved or defended it

Against: Anabaptists in Basel and its territory

Oecolampadius held disputations with the Anabaptists and supported the council's laws against them, which punished rebaptism with imprisonment and banishment. The council, not Oecolampadius, made and enforced the laws.[2][5]

Outcome: Anabaptists were jailed and expelled from Basel territory.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Oecolampadius was condemned by the Catholic majority at the Disputation of Baden and his removal was demanded, but the Basel council protected him. He was never arrested.

Excommunications

Oecolampadius left the Catholic Church and was treated as a heretic by Rome; the Disputation of Baden (1526) condemned his teaching and demanded his removal. No individual papal bull against him is recorded. His works were banned on the Catholic Index of Prohibited Books.

May–June 1526

Condemned at the Disputation of Baden

At a disputation organized by the Catholic cantons and the Bishop of Constance, with Johann Eck as chief Catholic speaker, the majority declared for the Catholic side, forbade religious innovations, excommunicated Zwingli, and called on Basel to remove Oecolampadius from his preaching office.[4][2]
