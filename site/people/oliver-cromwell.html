Oliver Cromwell

1599–1658 · Reformation

Also known as Lord Protector of the Commonwealth of England, Scotland and Ireland; 'Old Ironsides'

Puritan soldier and statesman; parliamentary general in the English Civil Wars and Lord Protector (1653–1658)

A Cambridgeshire gentleman and MP who, after an intense Puritan conversion, became the most successful general of the parliamentary side in the English Civil Wars. He helped bring Charles I to trial and execution (1649), conquered Ireland and Scotland, and ruled as Lord Protector from 1653. He gave wide liberty to Protestant sects and quietly allowed Jews to return to England, but banned the Prayer Book and Catholic worship. His army's massacres at Drogheda and Wexford (1649) and the confiscation of Catholic land in Ireland make him one of the most hated figures in Irish history.

What they did

  • Built the disciplined 'Ironsides' cavalry and helped create the New Model Army, which won the First Civil War for Parliament at Marston Moor and Naseby.[2][3]
  • Ruled England, Scotland, and Ireland as Lord Protector (1653–1658), the only non-royal head of state in English history.[3][8]
  • Under the Instrument of Government (1653) protected the worship of Protestants of many kinds outside a broad national church, including Independents, Baptists, and others, while excluding 'popery and prelacy'.[3][7]
  • Allowed Jews to live and worship privately in England from 1656, after the Whitehall Conference, called in answer to Menasseh ben Israel's petition, reached no formal decision; Jews had been expelled in 1290.[6][3]
  • Saw himself and his army as instruments of God's providence; his letters and speeches are among the fullest records of Puritan piety in politics.[1][7]
  • Drove the trial and execution of Charles I in 1649, an act he defended as God's judgment and his opponents called regicide.[1][2]
  • Conquered Ireland (1649–1650) and Scotland (1650–1651), uniting the three kingdoms under one government for the first time.[4][3]
  • Dissolved Parliaments that opposed him and ruled for a time through major-generals, leading critics then and since to call him a military dictator.[3][7]

Persecution they supported or carried out

Cromwell argued for liberty of conscience among Protestants and protected sects such as Baptists and, to a degree, Quakers. He excluded Catholics and Anglican episcopal worship from that liberty, and his campaigns in Ireland included massacres and a land settlement that dispossessed Catholic landowners. He sought leniency for the Quaker James Nayler in 1656 but did not stop Parliament's punishment.

11 September 1649

Massacre at Drogheda

Ordered or signed it

Against: The royalist garrison of Drogheda, Catholic priests and friars, and some civilians

After his summons to surrender was refused, Cromwell's army stormed Drogheda and, on his order of no quarter, killed most of the garrison of about 3,000 men, including the commander Sir Arthur Aston, and the Catholic clergy found in the town; survivors were shipped to Barbados. Cromwell's own letter calls it 'a righteous judgment of God'. How many civilians were killed is disputed: some historians, such as Tom Reilly, argue few were, while Micheál Ó Siochrú and others hold that many townspeople died.[1][4][5]

Outcome: About 3,500 killed by Cromwell's own estimate; the event became central to Irish memory of Cromwell.

11 October 1649

Sack of Wexford

Took part

Against: The garrison and townspeople of Wexford, including Franciscan friars

While surrender terms were being negotiated, Cromwell's troops broke into Wexford and killed some 2,000 soldiers and civilians, including friars. Cromwell had not ordered the assault but wrote that he did not intend to punish it, calling it God's just judgment.[1][4]

Outcome: The town was sacked and many drowned trying to flee.

1652–1654

Cromwellian settlement of Ireland

Approved or defended it

Against: Irish Catholic landowners and their families

The Act for the Settlement of Ireland (1652), passed by the Rump Parliament and carried out under the Protectorate, confiscated most Catholic-owned land; many owners were transplanted west of the Shannon to Connacht and Clare, and thousands of Irish were transported as indentured labourers to the Caribbean. Catholic worship was banned and priests were hunted, with a reward offered for them.[4][3]

Outcome: Catholic landownership in Ireland fell from about 60% before 1641 to under 10% by the 1660s.

September 1650

Scottish prisoners after Dunbar

Took part

Against: About 5,000 Scottish soldiers captured at Dunbar

Prisoners taken by Cromwell's army were marched south to Durham, where some 1,500 or more died of hunger and disease in captivity under Sir Arthur Haselrig; many survivors were sold into indentured labour in New England and elsewhere.[2][8]

Outcome: Remains of some of the prisoners were found in Durham in 2013.

1645–1660

Bans on Catholic and Prayer Book worship

Approved or defended it

Against: Catholics and Anglicans loyal to bishops and the Book of Common Prayer

The Long Parliament abolished the Prayer Book in 1645. Cromwell's Instrument of Government (1653) excluded 'popery and prelacy' from religious liberty, and a 1655 proclamation after the royalist Penruddock rising barred ejected Anglican clergy from teaching or preaching. Enforcement was uneven, and private Prayer Book services often went on.[3][7]

Outcome: Episcopal worship and Catholic Mass remained illegal until the Restoration of 1660.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Cromwell was never arrested or tried in life; he was a soldier in a civil war in which he was repeatedly at risk of death or, had Parliament lost, prosecution for treason. After the Restoration his body was exhumed and 'executed'.

Excommunications

Cromwell was never excommunicated by any church; he belonged to no single denomination and was a member of no church that disciplined him. He was posthumously attainted for high treason by Parliament in 1660, a civil, not church, sentence.

30 January 1661

Posthumous execution

On the twelfth anniversary of Charles I's execution, the restored monarchy's Parliament had the bodies of Cromwell, his son-in-law Henry Ireton, and the judge John Bradshaw dug up from Westminster Abbey, hanged in chains at Tyburn, and beheaded. Cromwell's head was set on a pole above Westminster Hall for about two decades.[2][7]
