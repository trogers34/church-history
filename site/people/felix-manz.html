Felix Manz

c. 1498–1527 · Reformation

Also known as Felix Mantz

Co-founder of the Swiss Brethren (Anabaptists); the first Anabaptist executed by a Protestant government

The son of a canon of Zürich's Grossmünster, Manz was a learned humanist and an early follower of Huldrych Zwingli. With Conrad Grebel and George Blaurock he broke with Zwingli over infant baptism and the authority of the city council in church matters, and in January 1525 the group performed the first believers' baptisms of the Reformation, founding the Swiss Brethren. Repeatedly jailed, he was drowned in the Limmat in 1527 under a law the Zürich council had made the year before, becoming the first Anabaptist put to death by a Protestant government.

What they did

  • Co-founded the Swiss Brethren with Conrad Grebel and George Blaurock, the first Anabaptist congregation of the Reformation and the ancestor of today's Mennonites, Amish, and Hutterite-linked traditions.[4][5]
  • Argued from the New Testament that baptism should follow personal faith and repentance, and that the church should be a voluntary community separate from the state.[1][4]
  • Wrote a 'Protestation' to the Zürich council (late 1524) defending believers' baptism, one of the earliest Anabaptist statements of the doctrine.[1]
  • Used his training in Hebrew to help translate and study the prophets with the Zürich reformers before the break with Zwingli.[5][3]
  • Preached and baptized in the countryside around Zürich, especially in Grüningen, despite repeated arrests.[1][4]
  • Is traditionally credited with the hymn 'Mit Lust so will ich singen', later printed in the Anabaptist hymnal Ausbund.[5]

Persecution they supported or carried out

No record of Manz supporting or carrying out persecution of anyone. The Swiss Brethren rejected the use of force by Christians, and Manz asked to be left free to preach rather than for any action against others.

Persecution, arrests, and executions

Manz was arrested repeatedly from 1525, imprisoned, and finally executed by the Protestant government of Zürich for rebaptizing, with Huldrych Zwingli's support. His mother and brother urged him at the river to stay faithful.

Excommunications

Manz was not excommunicated by a church court; he was condemned by the civil council of Zürich. In 2004 the Reformed Church of the Canton of Zürich held a service of reconciliation with Mennonites and expressed regret for the persecution of the Anabaptists, and a plaque marking Manz's execution was placed on the Limmat in 2004.

January–November 1525

Bans, arrests, and imprisonment

After the January 1525 disputation the Zürich council ordered all infants baptized within eight days, banned the group's meetings, and expelled non-citizens such as Blaurock. Manz, a citizen, was arrested several times for preaching and baptizing, and after the November 1525 disputation was held with Grebel and Blaurock.[1][4]

March 1526

Life sentence and escape

The council sentenced Manz, Grebel, Blaurock, and others to indefinite imprisonment on bread and water in the Witch Tower. On 7 March it decreed that anyone who rebaptized would be drowned. Manz and others escaped on 21 March.[1][4]

3 December 1526 – 5 January 1527

Recapture, trial, and execution

Captured with Blaurock in the Grüningen district, Manz was tried by the Zürich council and condemned for rebaptizing and for teaching that Christians should not bear the sword or hold office. On 5 January 1527 he was taken by boat onto the Limmat, bound, and drowned. Blaurock, not a citizen, was flogged through the streets and banished the same day. Zwingli did not oppose the sentence and had supported the policy behind it.[1][2][4]
